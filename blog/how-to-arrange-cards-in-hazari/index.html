How to Arrange Your Cards in Hazari: A Practical Guide to 3-3-3-4

Six practical principles for splitting 13 Hazari cards into groups, backed by what a full search chooses across 400 random hands.

Tags

  • Hazari
  • Card Games
  • Strategy
  • Arranging Cards

Published November 24, 2026.

Hazari is decided before the first round. You have 13 cards, you must split them into groups of 3, 3, 3 and 4, and the groups must run from strongest to weakest with the four-card group the weakest of all. There are about 200,000 ways to split a hand, roughly a tenth of them legal.

These principles cut that down to a handful. To check them, I had the game engine search every legal split of 400 random hands and looked at what the best ones have in common.

1. Aim for no weak groups

The weakest hand in Hazari is an Indi, a group with no pair, colour or run. In a random arrangement most groups are Indi: across 1,000 random legal arrangements, only about 1.2 of the four groups were a Pair or better on average.

In the best arrangement the search found, all four groups were a Pair or better in every one of the 400 hands. Making sure no group is a plain Indi is the single biggest gain available.

2. Lift your weakest group before you polish your strongest

Going from Indi to Pair is a huge step: a Pair beats about three hands in four. Going from a Run to a Colour Run barely matters, as both already beat most hands. The odds of each three-card hand show the returns shrink as hands get stronger. Spend cards where they raise a weak group first.

3. Do not automatically use a Troy

A Troy is the strongest group. It also uses three cards of one rank, which may be the cards you needed for a run or a pair elsewhere. In the searched arrangements, the first group was a Troy in about 29% of hands, a Colour Run in about 20% and a Run in about 50%. Holding three of a kind does not always mean using it as a Troy, because spreading those cards around can lift weaker groups instead.

4. Use the fourth card of the last group as your dump

The four-card group is judged by its best three cards. The fourth card does not weaken it, so put your awkward card there, the one that fits nowhere else. See the four-card group in detail.

5. Get the ace right

An ace is high in A-K-Q, which is the highest run, and low in A-2-3, which is the second highest. A run like 2-A-K does not count. A sequence of three cards that wraps around the ace is not a run, so do not build one.

6. Check the order before you reveal

Read your groups from first to last and ask: is each one at least as strong as the next? Is the four-card group the weakest, judging it by its best three cards? A wrong order is the most common reason for an invalid arrangement. The list of beginner mistakes has more.

What the best arrangements look like

  • Group 1: a Troy, Colour Run or Run in almost every hand.
  • Group 2: a Run in about 7 hands in 10, and a Colour in about 2 in 10.
  • Group 3: a Colour in about two thirds of hands, a Run in about one third.
  • Group 4: a Colour in about four hands in five, and a Pair in about one in six.

That is a model, not a promise. It values a group by how many random three-card hands it beats, and it treats opponents as unknown. Real players are not random, so use these as a starting point and adjust when you know how your table plays.
