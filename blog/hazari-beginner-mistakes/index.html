7 Mistakes New Hazari Players Make When Arranging Cards

Seven common errors when arranging Hazari cards, from putting the groups in the wrong order to getting the ace wrong, with how to avoid each.

Tags

  • Hazari
  • Card Games
  • Beginners
  • Rules

Published November 24, 2026.

Hazari rules are simple to state and easy to slip on. These are the seven mistakes that cost new players rounds, or cause an argument at the reveal.

1. Putting the groups in the wrong order

The groups must run from strongest to weakest. If your third group is stronger than your second, the arrangement is not legal. Read your groups from left to right before you reveal.

2. Making the four-card group too strong

The four-card group has to be the weakest of the four. Find its best three cards and compare them with your third group. If the four-card group wins that comparison, move cards around.

3. Thinking all four cards count

The four-card group is judged by its best three cards. The fourth card is a spare. It is a handy place for a card that fits nowhere else, and it can make the last pile contain more points without changing the strength.

4. Getting the ace wrong

An ace is high in A-K-Q, the highest run, and low in A-2-3, the second highest. A hand such as 2-A-K is not a run, because the sequence does not wrap around the ace.

5. Mixing up Colour and Run

  • A Colour is three cards of one suit that are not in sequence.
  • A Run is three cards in sequence, in mixed suits.
  • A Colour Run is both: a sequence in one suit, and it beats a plain Run.

A Colour is weaker than a Run, which surprises people who play poker, where a flush beats a straight in many versions.

6. Putting everything into one group

A monster first group with three Indi groups behind it looks strong and usually isn't. In 1,000 random legal arrangements, only 1.7% had all four groups at Pair or better, while a careful search reached that in every hand tested. Spread your strength: see why.

7. Counting the points wrongly

The ace, king, queen, jack and ten are worth 10 points each. The 2 through the 9 are worth 5 each. A round winner takes every card in that round, not just their own. After the deal, all points collected must total 360. If they do not, recount. The 360-point check explains the rule.

Settle the rest before you play

Direction of play, who leads, and what happens when two groups tie differ between tables. Agree on them before the first deal. The house rules checklist lists them all, and the Hazari rules guide has the standard version.
