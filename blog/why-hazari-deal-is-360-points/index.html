Why Every Hazari Deal Is Worth Exactly 360 Points

The arithmetic behind 360 points per deal, how it splits across the four rounds, and the one-line check that catches most Hazari scoring mistakes.

Tags

  • Hazari
  • Card Games
  • Scoring
  • Arithmetic

Published November 24, 2026.

Every deal of standard Hazari hands out exactly 360 points, no more and no less. That fixed total is the most useful fact in the scoring, because it lets you catch mistakes in seconds. Here is where it comes from.

The arithmetic

  • In each suit, the ace, king, queen, jack and ten are worth 10 points each. That is five cards and 50 points.
  • The 2 through the 9 are worth 5 points each. That is eight cards and 40 points.
  • One suit is worth 50 + 40 = 90 points.
  • Four suits are worth 4 × 90 = 360 points.

Put another way, 20 cards are worth 10 points (200 points) and 32 cards are worth 5 points (160 points). The full table is in the Hazari card points guide.

How the 360 points reach players

Each player arranges 13 cards into groups of 3, 3, 3 and 4. The groups are compared one round at a time, and the winner of a round takes every card played in it. The first three rounds each contain 12 cards (four players, three cards each). The last round contains 16 cards because each player puts down four.

The average card is worth 360 ÷ 52, a little under 7 points. So on average:

  • A 12-card round holds about 83 points.
  • The 16-card round holds about 111 points, nearly a third of the whole deal.

A real round can hold more or fewer points than the average, depending on which cards were played. The point is that the last round is the biggest prize, which is why the four-card group deserves more thought than its position suggests.

The one-line check

After each deal, add up the points every player collected. The answer must be 360. If it is not, someone miscounted, and you know it before the totals are written down.

If three players have 275 between them, the fourth must have 85. If the scores add up to 350, ten points went missing. A common slip is counting a ten as 5 points or a 9 as 10.

The scorekeeper uses this check. When a round does not add up to the round total, it asks you to confirm before saving instead of silently accepting it. It does not block you, because some tables play with different totals.

When 360 is not the total

Some tables use other point values or totals. The house rules checklist lists what to agree on before the first deal. If you change the card values, change the expected total with them.
