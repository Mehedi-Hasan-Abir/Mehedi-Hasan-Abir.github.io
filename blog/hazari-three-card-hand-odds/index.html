How Rare Is a Troy? The Odds of Every Three-Card Hand in Hazari

Exact odds for Troy, Colour Run, Run, Colour, Pair and Indi in Hazari, counted across all 22,100 three-card hands, plus a surprise about which is rarer.

  • Hazari

  • Odds

Published November 24, 2026.

In Hazari every group of cards is judged as a three-card hand. The order, from strongest to weakest, is Troy, Colour Run, Run, Colour, Pair and Indi. Knowing the order is easy. Knowing how rare each hand is tells you how much to trust a group when you arrange your cards.

I counted them. There are 22,100 different ways to pick three cards from a 52-card deck, and I ran every one of them through the same function the game engine uses to rank hands.

The numbers

  • Troy (three cards of one rank): 52 hands, 0.235%, about 1 in 425.
  • Colour Run (a sequence in one suit): 48 hands, 0.217%, about 1 in 460.
  • Run (a sequence in mixed suits): 720 hands, 3.258%, about 1 in 31.
  • Colour (one suit, no sequence): 1,096 hands, 4.959%, about 1 in 20.
  • Pair (two cards of one rank): 3,744 hands, 16.941%, about 1 in 6.
  • Indi (none of the above): 16,440 hands, 74.389%, about three in four.

The six counts add up to 22,100, which is a useful check on the counting.

A surprise: Colour Run is rarer than Troy

Hazari ranks a Troy above a Colour Run, but there are only 48 Colour Runs against 52 Troys. In a random three-card hand a Colour Run is slightly harder to get. The ranking is a convention of the game, not a ranking by rarity, so do not be surprised when a table argues about it.

The same counts appear in three-card poker, which uses the same six hands. That agreement is a good independent check that the counting is right.

What the odds mean for a group

Rank a hand by how many other three-card hands it beats and the picture becomes clearer:

  • A Pair beats every Indi, so it already beats about three hands in four.
  • A Colour beats about 91% of all hands.
  • A Run beats about 96%.
  • A Troy or Colour Run beats more than 99%.

These figures are the reason a plain Pair is a respectable group, and why the computer opponent in the scorekeeper treats the jump from Indi to Pair as worth far more than the jump from Run to Colour Run. The article on how the computer chooses its cards uses exactly this idea.

A limit of these numbers

These are odds for three random cards. In the real game you hold 13 cards and choose how to group them, so you can build a Pair or a Colour far more often than random chance suggests. The number of ways to arrange 13 cards shows how much choice you actually have.
